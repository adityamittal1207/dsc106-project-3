How we built the explorer

Design choices

We wanted to see when fires across the West were most intense and where they spread from hour to hour. At the checkpoint we tried separate views for the peak hour, the daily cycle, and the distance from each fire’s starting point. Those charts made individual patterns easier to see, but we kept having to look back and forth between the map and the time series to figure out where a spike came from. We put a moveable timeline directly below the map so readers can jump to a peak, then zoom in or select a named fire to see what was happening on the ground.

In Burning now, the warm colors show measured fire radiative power. We used a log scale so lower-power detections are still visible alongside the brightest ones, and we give detections with missing power their own color. In Spread by date, a purple-to-yellow scale shows when the satellite first detected fire at each location. Keeping those views separate lets readers follow the latest detections in one view and see the larger footprint build up in the other.

We grouped the five-minute scans by local solar hour so that the same hour means roughly the same sun position across the study area. Short-lived detections that didn’t recur within two hours were filtered out. Missing power for cloud-flagged pixels was estimated from nearby readings in the same hour for the timeline. The map shows measured power where it’s available. Try clicking a named fire, switching to Spread by date, and dragging the timeline from its first report through its peak to see where detections accumulated.

Development process

Steven Ngo worked on the GOES data and fire perimeters and contributed to the checkpoint charts. Aditya Mittal built exploratory views and turned the checkpoint into the final map and timeline. Leo Wong wrote the checkpoint explanation of the dataset, research question, and prototype, and put together the final write-up.

Our rough estimate is 25 person-hours in all: about eight each for Steven and Leo and nine for Aditya. The longest parts were preparing the satellite data for hourly comparisons and getting the map and timeline to stay in sync when switching fires or moving through time.
